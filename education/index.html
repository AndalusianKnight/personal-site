HIST E-1960 · History of the Cold War · 12 pp

Munitions of the Mind: Encryption, Export Controls and the End of the Cold War

For most of the Cold War, the United States treated strong encryption the way it treated missiles: as a munition, licensed for export and kept away from the Soviet bloc through CoCom, the Western export-control regime. This paper follows that logic from wartime codebreaking to the personal computer, when cryptography stopped being a state secret and started shipping inside ordinary software.

When the Soviet Union collapsed, the controls lost their enemy but kept their rules. What followed was the crypto wars of the 1990s, which decided whether ordinary people could own unbreakable encryption at all. I argue those fights were the Cold War's last policy argument, and the reason a company like Tauria could exist twenty years later.

COMPSCI 50 · Introduction to Computer Science · Flask · SQL · JS

DM Screen: a web app for running D&D at the table

My CS50 final project: everything a dungeon master juggles mid-session, in one browser tab. An initiative tracker, a dice roller with a full log, monster stat blocks, and session notes the players can't peek at.

Built with Python, Flask, SQL and JavaScript. The players' view and the DM's view are separate pages that share one game state, which taught me more about permissions than any lecture did. My players still say the dice roller is rigged. It isn't. Probably.

HIST E-1825 · Power and Civilization of China · 9 pp

Paper Promises: The Song Dynasty and the First Government Paper Money

In eleventh-century Sichuan, merchants were tired of hauling strings of heavy iron coins, so they started trading paper receipts called jiaozi. The Song state took the idea over and issued the world's first government paper money.

This paper looks at how the Song persuaded people to trust a piece of paper, and how later governments lost that trust by printing too much of it. Nine hundred years on, it's still the best case study I know of what happens when you spend trust faster than you earn it.
